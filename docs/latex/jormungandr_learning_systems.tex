\begin{abstract}
Jörmungandr is a distributed reinforcement-learning framework designed for
research and policy deployment. Applications collect experience through
external actors, while a central learner trains the policy. The trained
policy can then be exported for inference independently of the training
service. This arrangement allows an experiment to change its learning
algorithm while retaining the surrounding application.

The framework supports value-based and policy-gradient methods, including
algorithms for historical data and asynchronous collection. Distributional
critics provide estimates of return variation, and structured policies handle
variable sets of objects and feasible actions. We explain how these methods
use experience and how the service manages training, evaluation, and deployment.
The financial application uses Volt to define the portfolio decisions and
calculate their consequences.

Controlled studies examine algorithm behavior and the effect of structured
action representations. A search experiment also tests whether allocating
computation to diverse paths improves on selecting the highest estimates.
At a width of eight paths, mean regret fell from 0.1435 to 0.1305 across 500
paired synthetic trees, with additional selection cost. In a constrained
allocation task, imitation achieved 0.908 of the exact optimum; subsequent
PPO training raised the mean to 0.923. These results establish working
mechanisms on the tested problems and identify the larger application
comparisons still needed.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\begin{SNcolumns}

As we considered RL frameworks for research and production, we found that 
many libraries made it difficult to deploy without the overhead: drawing inferences
required a specialized runtime.  What we 
wanted was a clear separation between the agents generating experiences,
the reinforcement learning algorithm, and most critically the policy.  
For our purposes, the ability to checkpoint and deploy policy parameters without the 
overhead of the entire framework is critical for production use and forms a core
requirement for Jörmungandr.  We also wanted to be able to compare different 
algorithms and policies without having to change the underlying system.  
This paper describes the design and implementation of a plugin-based distributed 
reinforcement learning system that meets these requirements.

The design follows the way an experiment develops. An application first
defines its decisions and supplies the experience from which they can be
learned. A researcher can then compare learning methods on that experience,
examine their behavior on reserved episodes, and deploy a selected policy.
Keeping the application in place makes those comparisons easier to interpret:
a change in results can be investigated alongside the change in the learner.

The methods place different demands on the training data. A value-based
learner can reuse individual transitions from a replay buffer. PPO needs the
probabilities assigned to actions when they were collected, together with
ordered trajectories for estimating their advantages. A learned world model
adds an internal simulation through which a policy can be trained. Jörmungandr
accommodates these requirements through algorithm plugins, with the service
responsible for storing experience and managing model versions.

The paper develops this architecture before turning to the learning methods.
Their differences become especially relevant in financial applications, where
an action's return may be noisy and its consequences delayed. We use controlled
experiments to examine those issues and to test how the representation of
available actions affects learning. The final sections discuss the results
and their implications for larger trading problems.

\end{SNcolumns}

\section{System scope}

\subsection{Actors and the central learner}

An actor interacts with the application environment and records the outcome
of a chosen action. For a trading simulation, this includes observing the
portfolio, requesting a decision, and calculating the reward after applying
it. The actor sends that experience to the central learner, which maintains
the training state for the model. Figure~\ref{fig:system} shows the path from
collection to policy export.

Keeping the simulator in the actor allows collection to proceed across
several machines. The learner receives a common description of experience
from them, so an application can distribute its simulations while training
one policy.

A transition connects the observation before an action to the observation
and reward that follow it. Its episode and timestep locate it within the
sequence, while its policy version identifies the model that selected the
action. For stochastic policies, the actor also records the selected action's
probability and the estimated state value. These quantities are used later
when the learner reconstructs the update.

The application defines the action vocabulary. In a portfolio problem, a
slot might mean closing a position or changing a hedge. A legal-action mask
marks which slots are available in the current state. The replay buffer stores
the selected slot as an integer index, with the current and next masks aligned
to the same vocabulary.

\subsection{Hardware-aware inference and load balancing}

Concurrent actors can generate enough inference requests to benefit from
batching. The service combines requests with matching input settings and
assigns them to model replicas through a shared queue. At startup it detects
the available devices and allocates replicas up to the configured maximum.
Explicit settings can fix that allocation for a benchmark or memory budget.

A serving replica has a current model and a spare copy. New weights are loaded
into the spare copies before the replicas switch to the new policy version.
This allows requests already in progress to finish using the preceding version
while subsequent requests receive the fully loaded update. Structured on-policy
models refresh after each learner update; high-rate off-policy models can
refresh less often.

The balance between throughput and response time depends on how long requests
wait for a batch. The tuner measures a limited set of batching delays and
active-replica counts within the allocated pool. Among configurations within
five percent of the best throughput, it chooses the shortest delay and fewest
workers. Figure~\ref{fig:inference-load-balancing} shows how this serving path
relates to the learner.

For fixed-vector policies, a model may additionally declare windowed $X$ and
application state.  The runtime validates each shape and flattens in
the recorded $[X,\mathrm{state}]$ order before normalization.  Flat vectors of
the same width remain valid.  The layout records dimensions and flatten order;
the actor defines the window and its temporal meaning.

\subsection{Split isolation}

Training experience supplies the observation statistics used for normalization
and enters replay for subsequent updates. Validation experience uses those
training statistics in a separate evaluation store. This keeps evaluation
consistent with the preprocessing available to the trained policy.

The application assigns the split before an episode begins. In a financial
simulation, that usually means reserving complete paths. Individual steps
from one path share information, so dividing them between training and
validation would weaken the independence of the evaluation even if their
storage were separate.

\subsection{Trajectory construction}

PPO and related methods estimate returns from a sequence of decisions. To
prepare those sequences, the service groups records by actor and episode,
then orders them by timestep. A missing step or an episode boundary ends the
fragment. When old data have been overwritten in circular replay, the retained
suffix forms a new fragment beginning at its first available observation.

Policy age also affects which fragments are useful. Online trajectory methods
accept experience within a configured lag from the current policy. Historical
demonstrations remain available to MARWIL, whose objective is designed to learn
from them. These selection rules allow the service to prepare the data in the
form required by the chosen algorithm.

\section{Plugin and artifact model}

\begin{SNcolumns}

\subsection{Algorithm interface}

The plugin contains the model and the procedure for updating it. It accepts
batches prepared by the service, performs the learning step, and returns enough
information for replay priorities and training reports to be updated. An update
has the form
\[
  U=(\ell,p_1,\ldots,p_B,\mathcal M),
\]
where $\ell$ is the loss and $p_i$ is the priority statistic for sampled row
$i$. The additional metrics $\mathcal M$ describe the algorithm's internal
behavior. For example, a value learner may report its prediction residuals,
while a stochastic policy also reports entropy.

Sampling is implemented through a separate selector plugin. This allows an
experiment to change how experience is chosen while keeping the learning
objective fixed. External algorithms and selectors register through Python
entry points, alongside the built-in implementations.

\subsection{Checkpoint lifecycle}

A checkpoint must contain enough information to resume the calculation that
produced the policy. Along with model weights, it stores the optimizer state
and the preprocessing used for observations. The saved configuration identifies
the algorithm version and action vocabulary, so restoration can check that
the receiving model interprets the parameters correctly. Changes to a plugin's
state format require a compatible version or an explicit migration.

Replay is held in memory. Restoring a checkpoint resumes the learner with
empty experience stores, after which actors supply new data. For deployment,
the exporter produces the policy with a description of its input and output.
A distributional model retains the meaning of its return estimates; a
policy model exposes its action scores.

TorchScript provides the standard export route. Fixed-vector policies can
also be exported to ONNX with normalization embedded in the model. Before
publication, the exported outputs are compared with the training implementation
under specified numerical tolerances. This connects the deployment requirement
in the introduction to a concrete check: the application receives the policy
that was evaluated, with the same preprocessing.

Checkpoint loading uses trusted local storage because the Torch format relies
on pickle. The service's fixed-vector interface also has an SBE schema for
compact transport; structured messages use bounded JSON requests with gzip
compression.

\end{SNcolumns}

\subsection{Value distributions}

A scalar value function estimates the expected return from an action. In
financial problems, the range of outcomes can matter as much as the mean:
two actions with similar expected reward may have quite different downside.
C51 represents that range through learned probabilities on fixed return
points~\cite{c51}. QR-DQN learns the locations $\theta_i(s,a)$ of specified
quantiles $\tau_i$~\cite{qrdqn}. For target samples $y_j$, its quantile-Huber
loss is

\[
  \mathcal L=
  \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}
  \left|\tau_i-\mathbf 1\{y_j-\theta_i<0\}\right|
  L_\kappa(y_j-\theta_i).
\]

The quantiles can be averaged for expected-return control. A downside rule
can use a lower quantile or average the lowest fraction to obtain an empirical
lower-tail CVaR score. Such a rule makes the risk preference explicit in action
selection. Its usefulness depends on how well the estimated tails describe
the evaluation population, particularly under a change in market conditions.
The distribution describes variation in outcomes; uncertainty about the fitted
model needs its own assessment.

DQN provides the scalar baseline. The Double-DQN construction uses separate
selection and evaluation calculations to reduce the optimism introduced by
maximizing noisy value estimates~\cite{dqn,doubleq}. Historical data raise a
further concern: an action with little representation in the data may receive
an unjustifiably high value. The discrete CQL implementation adds a conservative
penalty to address this problem~\cite{cql}.

\subsection{Policy, asynchronous, and offline objectives}

PPO adjusts the probability of actions according to their estimated advantage
over the value of the state. Generalized Advantage Estimation controls how
reward information from different horizons contributes to that estimate~\cite{ppo,gae}.
The clipped objective limits the incentive for large changes in action
probability during repeated updates from the same data.

When collection and learning proceed asynchronously, an actor may use an older
policy. IMPALA accounts for that difference through V-trace corrections~\cite{impala}.
The APPO implementation follows IMPACT, combining a target policy with
truncated importance sampling and bounded circular replay~\cite{impact}.
This makes the age and producing policy of a trajectory part of the learning
calculation.

A shared actor--critic network introduces another route for policy change:
training the value function can modify features also used to select actions.
Structured PPO provides an optional scale for those critic gradients. It also
offers a check on the action-probability ratios after a proposed update. If a
proposal exceeds the declared bounds, the learner restores the preceding
state and retries at a lower learning rate. Both controls are disabled by
default and can be assessed independently in an experiment.

Demonstrations provide a different starting point. Behavior cloning fits the
observed choices directly, with weights available for balancing examples.
MARWIL uses estimated trajectory advantages to weight the likelihood of
historical actions, giving better-than-expected outcomes greater influence~\cite{marwil}.
The resulting policy depends on the coverage and quality of the demonstrations,
so its evaluation should include the situations in which it will be deployed.

\subsection{Entropy and world models}

The maximum-entropy objective augments discounted reward with policy entropy,

\[
 J_\alpha(\pi)=\E_\pi\left[
   \sum_{t\geq0}\gamma^t
   \left(r(s_t,a_t)+\alpha\mathcal H(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t))\right)
 \right].
\]

Here $\alpha$ determines the value assigned to policy entropy. The soft-Q
implementation incorporates this term in its value backup. Categorical SAC
uses a learned action distribution and twin critics, with an option to adjust
$\alpha$ during training~\cite{sac}. Legal-action masks restrict the distribution
to feasible choices, and the entropy calculation uses the limit $0\log0=0$
for masked actions.

A world-model method takes a further step by learning how the environment
evolves and training through imagined trajectories. DreamerV3 demonstrates
this approach across a broad set of tasks~\cite{dreamerv3}. Jörmungandr includes
a compact profile for vector observations, with learned latent dynamics and
predictions of reward and continuation. Its imagined trajectories support
actor and value updates. Reproducing the full published DreamerV3 system would
require the reference representation and training procedure, including its
recurrent state-space model and pixel decoder.

\section{Noise, disturbance, and risk}

\begin{SNcolumns}

\subsection{Choosing a disturbance model}

Robustness is easiest to assess when an experiment identifies where the
uncertainty enters. Observation noise changes the information available to
the policy. Random transitions change the outcome of an otherwise identical
choice. A learned world model introduces prediction error that can accumulate
along an imagined path. Each case suggests a different evaluation.

The runtime supports normalization based on training data and optional
Gaussian perturbations of observations. Value profiles use Huber losses and
can clip gradients to reduce the influence of large residuals. Reward clipping
is also available, although it changes the objective by changing the relative
importance of outcomes. Those settings are retained with the model so their
effect can be traced through evaluation.

The algorithm choice follows the source of error. Distributional critics are
useful for describing return variation. Conservative value learning addresses
weak coverage of actions in historical data, while V-trace addresses the age
of the policy that collected a trajectory. An experiment can combine these
questions, provided it reports the perturbation and performance measure used
for each comparison.

\subsection{What maximum entropy proves}

Eysenbach and Levine relate maximum-entropy reinforcement learning to a lower
bound on a robust objective for specified reward and transition disturbances~\cite{maxentrobust}.
Their result motivates testing whether a policy that retains several viable
behaviors is less sensitive to the disturbances in an application. The scope
of that test is set by the disturbance family and the entropy coefficient.

For the financial application, a comparison can begin with DQN or PPO and
then vary the decision rule for a distributional critic. A fixed downside
rule tests the effect of risk preference; varying the entropy coefficient
tests the value of retaining a broader action distribution. Using the same
paths makes the differences easier to attribute to those choices. Chronological
splits and settings chosen before evaluation preserve the distinction between
development and the subsequent test.

\end{SNcolumns}

\section{QUBO rollout and frontier allocation}
\label{sec:qubo}

\subsection{The budgeted selection problem}

The computational problem is deciding which paths merit an expensive evaluation
before their scores are known.  The service must make that decision
using information that is already cheap.  Let $\mathcal{B}_{t-1}$ be the beam
retained at search depth $t-1$, and let

\[
 \mathcal{C}_t
 =\bigcup_{v\in\mathcal{B}_{t-1}}\operatorname{child}(v)
 =\{c_1,\ldots,c_{n_t}\}
\]

be the next candidate frontier.  Candidate $c_i$ has a cheap information set
$\mathcal{I}_{t,i}$---for example a policy/value estimate, an admissible bound,
a shallow rollout, or model disagreement---and an expensive terminal
value $Y_i$ that remains to be evaluated.  A binary decision $x_i=1$ retains the candidate.  With a beam
budget $k_t$, the ideal one-step search decision is

\[
 \underset{x\in\{0,1\}^{n_t}}{\operatorname{arg\,max}}
 \quad
 \E\!\left[
   \max_{i:x_i=1}Y_i\,\middle|\,\mathcal{I}_t
 \right]
 \qquad
 \text{subject to}\qquad
 \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}x=k_t .
 \tag{1}
\]

The conditional joint distribution of the $Y_i$ is generally unavailable.
Moreover, selecting the individually largest point estimates can spend the
entire beam on highly correlated siblings whose errors share the same cause.
The implemented QUBO is therefore a tractable surrogate for (1): reward a
cheap utility estimate while charging for pairwise redundancy.  The recursion
is

\[
 \mathcal{B}_{t}=\{c_i\in\mathcal{C}_t:x_{t,i}=1\},
 \qquad \mathcal{B}_0=\{\text{root}\},
 \tag{2}
\]

and only $\mathcal{B}_D$ receives terminal evaluation at depth $D$.  The same
subset problem appears later in the learning pipeline when $c_i$ is a replay
transition or contiguous rollout fragment and $Y_i$ denotes the learning value
of evaluating that transition or fragment; terminal path value is a different
quantity.  Search pruning is potentially more
valuable because it occurs before the expensive work.

Figure~\ref{fig:qubo-frontier-trace} makes the recursion explicit.  The retained
parents are first expanded, and one QUBO is formed over the complete frontier
$\mathcal{C}_t$.  Its single decision vector determines the next beam.

\subsection{Utility and redundancy}

Let $\widehat v_i$ be the available scalar utility.  To prevent isolated
outliers from determining the linear QUBO coefficient, the implementation uses
the fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles $q_{.05}$ and $q_{.95}$:

\[
 u_i=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
   \frac{\widehat v_i-q_{.05}}{q_{.95}-q_{.05}},0,1
 \right).
 \tag{3}
\]

If the percentile range is numerically zero, each candidate receives
$u_i=\tfrac12$.  Non-finite values are replaced by the finite median before
scaling.  In replay mode, the default unscaled utility is
$\widehat v_i=\operatorname{mean}_{j\in c_i}\log(1+p_j)$ for replay priority
$p_j$.  Absolute reward is an optional addition. It is disabled by default because
selecting large realized rewards can favor lucky outcomes in noisy data.

Each candidate also has an embedding $z_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.  For coordinate
$r$, define its median $m_r$, interquartile range $a_r$, and stabilized scale
$\bar a_r=a_r$ when $a_r>10^{-8}$ and $\bar a_r=1$ otherwise.  The robust
distance and RBF similarity are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \widetilde z_{ir} &= \frac{z_{ir}-m_r}{\bar a_r}, \\
 d_{ij}^{2} &= \lVert\widetilde z_i-\widetilde z_j\rVert_2^2, \\
 h &= \operatorname{median}\{d_{ij}^{2}:d_{ij}^{2}>10^{-12}\}, \\
 s_{ij} &= \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^{2}}{2h}\right),
 \qquad s_{ii}=0 .
\end{aligned}
 \tag{4}
\end{equation}

The degenerate bandwidth is set to one.  Transition embeddings contain the
current state, state delta, action, reward, and terminal flag.  Rollout
embeddings summarize state and delta plus action, reward, terminal, and length.
In the path experiment below, a one-hot branch-position embedding makes paths
with long common prefixes similar.

\subsection{Constrained subset objective and QUBO matrix}

For $n$ candidates and target cardinality $k$, the binary energy is

\[
 E(x)=
 -\lambda_u\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_i x_i
 +\lambda_s\sum_{i<j}s_{ij}x_ix_j
 +\lambda_k\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i-k\right)^2,
 \qquad x_i\in\{0,1\}.
 \tag{5}
\]

The first term favors utility, the second charges for redundant pairs, and the
third turns the cardinality constraint into an unconstrained binary penalty.
Using $x_i^2=x_i$ and a symmetric convention $x^{\mathsf T}Qx$, expansion of
the final term gives

\[
 Q_{ii}=-\lambda_u u_i+\lambda_k(1-2k),
 \qquad
 Q_{ij}=\frac{\lambda_s s_{ij}+2\lambda_k}{2}\quad(i\ne j),
 \tag{6}
\]

such that $x^{\mathsf T}Qx=E(x)-\lambda_k k^2$.  The omitted constant cannot
change the minimizer.  Equation (6) is the exported Q matrix consumed by an
external annealer or binary optimizer.  An unconstrained backend must validate
that $\lambda_k$ is strong enough for its coefficient scale.  The built-in
solver instead stays in the feasible set $\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}x=k$, so its
cardinality is exact and the penalty is constant.

Within that feasible set, minimizing (5) is equivalent to maximizing

\[
 F(S)=\lambda_u\sum_{i\in S}u_i
      -\lambda_s\sum_{\substack{i<j\\i,j\in S}}s_{ij},
 \qquad |S|=k .
 \tag{7}
\]

The classical reference solver greedily adds the candidate with largest
marginal score

\[
 \Delta_i(S)=\lambda_u u_i-\lambda_s\sum_{j\in S}s_{ij},
 \tag{8}
\]

then applies deterministic one-for-one exchanges.  Replacing selected item
$o$ by unselected item $n$ changes (7) by

\[
 \Delta F_{o\rightarrow n}
 =\lambda_u(u_n-u_o)
 -\lambda_s\!\left(
   \sum_{j\in S\setminus\{o\}}s_{nj}
   -\sum_{j\in S\setminus\{o\}}s_{oj}
 \right).
 \tag{9}
\]

Positive exchanges are accepted until no one-swap improvement remains or the
configured pass limit is reached.  The implementation uses a classical
one-swap local heuristic, and the reported result covers only that solver.
Q construction requires quadratic similarity
storage; the candidate-pool factor bounds $n$ in replay, and the beam bounds it
during search.  Candidate identities, decisions, energy, selected utility,
redundancy, matrix bytes, and solve time remain auditable.

The formulation follows QUBO episode selection \cite{quboepisodes};
quantum-inspired priority mechanisms provide a related replay direction
\cite{quantumreplay}.  The experiment here uses a classical solver.
When this selector follows prioritized sampling, it changes the probability
that an item reaches the learner. The implementation therefore uses neutral
learner weights; correcting for the combined selection probability remains
an open part of the sampling procedure.

\subsection{Controlled branching experiment}

The empirical question is whether the diversity term can preserve a good
region under correlated heuristic error, and whether the saved evaluation
work can repay solve overhead.  Each of 500 paired trials constructs a complete
four-way tree of depth six.  A path is
$p=(a_1,\ldots,a_\ell)$ with $a_t\in\{0,1,2,3\}$.  Independent edge increments
at depth $t$ satisfy

\[
 \xi_{p_{1:t}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/t),
 \qquad
 Y_p=\sum_{t=1}^{6}\xi_{p_{1:t}}
 \quad\text{for }|p|=6 .
 \tag{10}
\]

Thus the standard deviation of an increment is $1/\sqrt{t}$.  For a
prefix $p$, the benchmark oracle computes

\[
 M_p=\max_{q:\,p\text{ is a prefix of }q}Y_q .
 \tag{11}
\]

The search uses a perturbed estimate of $M_p$ as its cheap score:

\[
 \widehat v_p=M_p+B_{a_1}+\varepsilon_p,
 \qquad
 B_a\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
 \qquad
 \varepsilon_p\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(0.35\sigma)^2),
 \quad \sigma=0.9 .
 \tag{12}
\]

$B_{a_1}$ is shared by all paths in a first-level region and therefore creates
the correlated optimism that diversity is intended to hedge;
$\varepsilon_p$ is independent.  The proxy is intentionally derived from the
oracle plus controlled noise.  It isolates subset selection under an
oracle-derived noisy proxy; production value-model accuracy is outside this
claim.

The tree contains $4^6=4{,}096$ terminal paths and
$\sum_{t=1}^{6}4^t=5{,}460$ non-root candidates.  Trial seeds are
$20{,}260{,}802+s$ for $s=0,\ldots,499$.  Utility-only search retains the top
$k$ values of $\widehat v$; the robust scaling preserves that order apart from ties introduced by clipping.
QUBO uses $\lambda_u=1$, $\lambda_s=0.12$, $\lambda_k=4$, and four local-swap
passes.  Beam widths $k\in\{4,6,8,10,12,16\}$ are evaluated on identical trees.

For terminal beam $\mathcal{B}_6$, oracle value $Y^*=M_{\emptyset}$, and
selected value $\widehat Y=\max_{p\in\mathcal{B}_6}Y_p$, the primary metrics are

\[
 R=Y^*-\widehat Y,
 \qquad
 H=\mathbf{1}\{R\le 10^{-12}\},
 \qquad
 G=\sum_{t=1}^{6}|\mathcal{C}_t|,
 \qquad
 L=|\mathcal{B}_6| .
 \tag{13}
\]

$R$ is simple terminal regret, $H$ is exact-optimum recovery, $G$ counts cheap
candidate generations, and $L$ counts terminal evaluations.  Mean-regret bars
use normal 95\% intervals; recovery bars use 95\% Wilson intervals.  Because
selectors see the same tree in each trial, paired regret improvement is
$D_s=R_{s,\mathrm{utility}}-R_{s,\mathrm{QUBO}}$ with interval

\[
 \overline D\ \pm\ 1.96\,s_D/\sqrt{500} .
 \tag{14}
\]

The run used one Python 3.11.15 process with NumPy 1.26 on an AMD Ryzen
Threadripper PRO 7985WX.  Timing is machine-specific; oracle construction is
evaluation instrumentation and is excluded from search accounting.

\subsection{Compute break-even and interpretation}

Let $c_g$ be the average cost of generating and cheaply scoring one candidate,
$c_e$ the average terminal-evaluation cost, and $T_{s,A}$ selector time for
method $A$.  Its accounted search cost is

\[
 T_A=T_{s,A}+c_gG_A+c_eL_A .
 \tag{15}
\]

QUBO width eight is close in aggregate quality to utility width ten.  QUBO
uses $G_Q=148$ candidates and $L_Q=8$ terminal evaluations; utility width ten
uses $G_U=180$ and $L_U=10$.  QUBO is faster whenever

\[
 32c_g+2c_e>T_{s,Q}-T_{s,U}\approx2.75\text{ ms} .
 \tag{16}
\]

If candidate generation is treated as free, Equation~(16) requires
$c_e>1.38$ ms per avoided terminal evaluation.  Any nonzero $c_g$ lowers that
threshold.  Conversely, if $\widehat v_i$ itself requires the full rollout,
the optimization is too late to recover rollout cost.

The benefit in this experiment comes from preserving a broader set of paths
under correlated estimation error. Its practical value depends on obtaining
the cheap estimate before the expensive calculation and on recovering the
selection overhead through avoided evaluations. A financial application
should therefore measure the complete cost in Equation~(15) on fixed path
sets, using estimates available at the time of search. Varying the diversity
coefficient would show how sensitive the gain is to the allocation rule.
The present evidence concerns a classical selector in a synthetic search;
testing it within policy training is a further experiment.

\section{Policy representation and Volt}

\subsection{Fixed embeddings and end-to-end encoders}

A financial policy acts on a representation of a portfolio. Volt defines the
contracts and calculates the consequences of changing holdings, including
the resulting reward. It also supplies the feasible actions. Jörmungandr
learns how to choose among those actions from the information available at
the decision time.

The number of positions and candidate trades can change between observations.
Deep Sets offers a representation whose meaning is preserved under a
permutation of the objects~\cite{deepsets}. A graph network can additionally
represent relationships between them~\cite{graphnet}. Either encoder can be
frozen to produce fixed-size inputs for a learner. Training the encoder jointly
with the policy allows its representation to develop around the decision
objective.

The structured interface carries a global observation together with a variable
set of typed entities and candidate actions. Padding is introduced when
observations are collected into a batch. DQN uses the selected candidate in
transition replay, while PPO receives a sequence of decisions. For a portfolio
turn involving several choices, those choices form one joint action with one
environment reward.

This joint action is sampled sequentially. An earlier selection may consume
capacity and restrict the alternatives available to a later selection. The
actor therefore records the conditional probability used at each stage. The
sum of their logarithms gives the joint log probability required by PPO.
The recorded prefix and masks allow the learner to reconstruct the distribution
from which the action was sampled. Revising an earlier mask after selection
would describe a different distribution, so such records are rejected.

The same sequence can be transported compactly as $N+1$ observations with
$N$ action-and-reward records. This avoids repeating an intermediate observation
as both the end of one transition and the start of the next. Compression
further reduces transport size. The current structured PPO path accepts
complete episodes; support for partial fragments would also require the
appropriate value estimate at the fragment boundary.

Demonstrations can initialize this policy before reinforcement learning.
For example $i$, let $C_i$ be the feasible candidates and $a_i$ the demonstrated
choice in state $s_i$. With a positive example weight $w_i$, behavior cloning
minimizes
\[
  \mathcal L_{\mathrm{BC}} =
  \frac{\sum_i w_i[-\log \pi_\theta(a_i\mid s_i,C_i)]}
       {\sum_i w_i}.
\]

Weights can compensate for uneven representation of decision types. The
application defines the groups, and the learner can assign weights proportional
to $\operatorname{count}(g_i)^{-\alpha}$ for $0\leq\alpha\leq1$, normalized to
mean one. Uniform sampling applies those weights in the loss. Alternatively,
sampling with probability $w_i/\sum_jw_j$ and using unit loss weights gives the
same expected objective while bringing rare, highly weighted examples into
more batches. The experiment in Section~8 examines this finite-batch effect.

Candidate identities keep the target aligned when rows are reordered.
Validation labels enter their own store and measure the accuracy of the
resulting choices. Because behavior cloning and structured PPO share the
policy representation, the fitted policy can initialize a PPO learner with
fresh optimizer state. The value function then learns from returns. Scaling
its gradients into the shared representation can help preserve the imitated
policy during this initial value-fitting stage.

\subsection{Quantile tree baselines}

A frozen portfolio encoder also makes tree-based comparisons possible.
CatBoost supports several quantiles in a single CPU model, while LightGBM
normally fits one model per quantile level~\cite{catboost,lightgbm,catboostdocs,lightgbmdocs}.
They provide relatively fast comparisons on the same numeric features.
The encoder stays fixed during tree training. Joint representation learning
uses Torch, where gradients can propagate from the policy or value loss
through the set or graph encoder.

\section{Metrics and model comparison}

The purpose of comparison is to choose a policy that performs well in its
environment. Training losses help explain how a method is learning, although
their scales depend on the objective. A CQL loss and a PPO loss therefore
need an external performance measure before they can inform a choice between
policies.

The service associates training statistics and supplied evaluation results
with the model version that produced them. The history can be examined through
the API or TensorBoard. A researcher can follow a change in environment reward
back to the corresponding update and inspect the algorithm's internal measures.

For a trading application, the evaluation should reflect the intended use.
A return measure can be accompanied by drawdown or turnover where those affect
the strategy's usefulness. Computational cost also matters when a decision
must be made within a fixed interval. We use matched interaction budgets and
reserved episodes in the comparisons below, with repeated training runs to
show sensitivity to initialization.

\subsection{Learning from demonstrations}

Before using demonstrations to initialize PPO, we checked that the structured
learner could fit a small set of labeled decisions and transfer the resulting
policy. Eight weighted labels from two factor families were presented under
four candidate permutations. The learner fitted the labels exactly, and
reordering candidates preserved their scores within $10^{-5}$.
A copy of the fitted policy initialized PPO with identical action scores and
fresh optimizer state.

Fitting labels is only one part of using a teacher. The demonstrations must
also respond appropriately to the information in the observation. The tools
for examining a teacher therefore compare its decisions across differing
inputs and inspect how those decisions are produced. Application-owned
counterfactual changes offer a further check: change a feature that should
matter and examine the response. Interpreting that response requires knowledge
of the task, since an unchanged action may be appropriate over a range of
states.

Some choices are better calculated exactly once their objective is known.
The library includes a bipartite assignment solver and a shortest-route
solver for positive-cost edges. An application can use them beneath a learned
task choice, supplying the feasible assignments or traversable graph. Optional
assignments with zero or negative improvement are omitted under the
maximum-utility rule. This lets learning address the uncertain decision while
an exact method carries out the specified combinatorial calculation.

The representation can also give each candidate direct access to the entities
relevant to it through cross-attention. In a six-worker routing example with
unseen validation worlds and matched training batches, pooled context achieved
98.4375\% accuracy and one candidate-attention block achieved 100\%.
The gain in this small task motivates examining whether an
application's candidate representation exposes the relationships needed for
its decision.

\subsection{Representing rare examples in a training batch}

The sampling comparison uses a fixed synthetic corpus so that batch quality
can be measured directly. It has 1,000 records
in one common balance stratum and ten records in each of two rare strata.
Square-root inverse-frequency balancing gives mean-one weights 0.85 and 8.5;
both arms therefore target the same exact weighted diagnostic objective,
0.2916667.

Across 3,000 deterministic batches of size 64, uniform draws followed by
weighted loss contain 1.2617 rare records on average and contain none in
26.07\% of batches.  Their objective estimate has mean absolute error
0.150488.  Weight-proportional draws with unit post-draw loss weights contain
10.6707 rare records on average, never omit them, and reduce mean absolute
error to 0.056728; absolute bias from the exact objective is 0.000639.  The improvement concerns estimation of the specified weighted objective.
Testing whether it also accelerates a policy requires a learning experiment
with the application's choice of balance groups.

\subsection{Conditioning a choice on earlier selections}

In a joint action, an earlier selection can change which later option is
preferable even when both remain feasible. The structured prefix head gives
the policy access to those earlier choices. One central inference emits a base logit $\ell_j$ and
key/value vectors $(k_j,v_j)$ for each candidate.  Before factor $i$, the
conditional logit for current candidate $j$ is
\[
  \ell_{j\mid p_{<i}} = \ell_j
  + \frac{k_j^\top\sum_{a\in p_{<i}}v_a}{\sqrt{d_p}}.
\]
The environment-owned callback still supplies the legal mask.  The actor and
PPO recompute the same expression from the recorded prefix, so a single service inference supplies the terms needed for the joint
probability. Setting $d_p=0$ gives a policy with prefix-independent scores.

The isolated \texttt{PrefixPreferenceGate-v0} contains two balanced labels
with an identical observation, identical current candidates, and identical
legal mask.  The correct quantity is small after a \texttt{wait} prefix and
large after a \texttt{produce} prefix.  Across five fixed seeds, the
prefix-independent arm stays at accuracy $0.5$ and median NLL $0.693147$, its
binary information limit.  A width-16 model with $d_p=4$ reaches accuracy one
on all five seeds and median NLL $0.009255$. The labels depend only on the
earlier selection, which isolates the information contributed by the prefix.
The result demonstrates the mechanism on this two-label problem.

\section{Serving policies and native computation}

The deployment requirement in the introduction favors a small inference path.

The current runtime also supports native episode learners through a resident
subprocess. A Volt/Burn backend uses this route for the companion
stock-and-option study. The service manages the worker lifecycle and exchanges
versioned requests with it. This provides an application-specific native
calculation alongside the Torch algorithm collection.

Further native work should follow measurements of where time is spent.
Repeated replay scans or graph collation may justify a compiled implementation
before the policy calculation itself does. Such a change can be checked against
the reference using the same inputs and saved model state. This keeps the
performance question tied to an identifiable part of the calculation.

\section{Current implementation and limits}

The comparisons above establish several working parts of the learning and
serving path. Further checks examine failures that can be obscured by aggregate
training statistics. One arises when rewards are concentrated at the end of a
long episode: the value function may fit the terminal outcome while the
opening actions receive only a very small advantage signal.

The learner therefore records the spread of episode returns and the fraction
of nonzero rewards. For GAE, the decay factor $\gamma\lambda$ and the
oldest-residual weight $(\gamma\lambda)^{N-1}$ help explain how strongly a
terminal outcome reaches the beginning of an episode. These quantities connect
the reward schedule to the update actually seen by the policy.

The frozen \texttt{DelayedTerminalCredit-v0} gate checks this exact mechanism
on balanced $-1/+1$ terminal rewards over 719 structured steps with zero
behavior values.  Jormungandr and Stable-Baselines3 2.9.0 each compute both
$\lambda=0.98$ and $\lambda=1$.  The raw targets are checked against
$z(\gamma\lambda)^d$; maximum disagreement between implementations is
$4.7684\times10^{-7}$, within the declared $2\times10^{-6}$ tolerance.  After
the same batch normalization used by PPO, the positive opening advantage is
$2.6763\times10^{-6}$ in the first arm and one in the episodic arm.  This calculation led to the choice of $\lambda=1$ for the subsequent
terminal-reward experiment. The comparison establishes the calculated targets;
their effect on learning must be measured through training.

The independent \texttt{StructuredPPOSafety-v0} gate tests policy preservation
over five seeds.  In a value-only joint update, the fully shared critic
moves a policy-backbone parameter by about $0.0200$.  Zero critic-backbone
scale leaves the non-value parameters bit-identical while the value head still
moves by about $0.0200$.  A second deliberately oversized actor proposal leaves
the frozen ratio interval $[0.5,2]$ in all unguarded seeds, reaching
$2.061\times10^{-9}$.  Transactional backoff accepts the third common-random
proposal in all five runs at effective rate $0.01$; all committed ratios lie in
$[0.670,1.518]$ or a tighter interval.  The controls prevented the deliberately induced policy changes in this test.
An environment comparison is needed to determine whether that preservation
also improves reward.

The software tests exercise a model through updating, saving, and restoring
it for inference. More specific cases check return calculations and action
identity under candidate reordering. They also verify the separation of
training and validation and the transfer from behavior cloning to PPO.
Together these checks support repeatable experiments with the implemented
methods.

The scope of the empirical results remains narrower than the algorithm
catalog. CQL and SAC are implemented for discrete actions, and the world-model
profile is smaller than the published DreamerV3 system. The experiments here
use compact environments whose decisions can be examined directly. Broader
benchmark comparisons and financial studies would be needed to assess their
relative performance in those settings.

There are operational limits as well. Replay is held in memory and is lost
on restart. Structured PPO accepts complete episodes, with partial-fragment
support still requiring appropriate boundary values. The application supplies
feasibility rules and executes the chosen actions. Multi-learner synchronization
and authentication remain outside the current service, and the QUBO selector
still needs a Volt-scale evaluation.

\section{Conclusion}

Jörmungandr provides a common setting in which learning methods can be
compared and a selected policy can be deployed. The application continues to
define its decisions and calculate their consequences. This is particularly
useful for the financial setting, where the interpretation of a trade belongs
with the portfolio model and its market assumptions.

The experiments illustrate how algorithm choice interacts with data and action
representation. Several methods learned the synthetic spread task, while the
structured comparisons showed that variable candidates and feasibility masks
can be carried through training. Demonstrations provided a useful starting
point in the constrained allocation problem. Search diversity also improved
average quality under correlated estimation error, at a measurable cost.
These findings motivate larger financial comparisons in which policy quality
and computation are assessed together.
