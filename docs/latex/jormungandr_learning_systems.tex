\begin{abstract}
Learning to make decisions requires more than selecting an algorithm. The
learner needs relevant observations, outcomes attached to the right actions,
and an evaluation that measures useful behavior. This paper connects those
requirements to prior work in reinforcement learning and examines them through
a series of controlled studies.

Selecting diverse search paths modestly improved average outcomes under
correlated estimation errors, but added computation. Structured action
representations preserved learning on a simple control task, and demonstrations
helped initialize decisions subject to shared constraints. A small language
model learned to choose services from recent verified history without showing
a general advantage over simpler rules. Additional prediction objectives
brought little benefit in the tested numeric tasks. Longer returns improved
early learning with delayed rewards, while explicit comparisons recovered
simulated preferences more reliably than position-biased clicks.

Together these results show how the information, evaluation budget, and
feedback supplied to a learner affect its behavior. They also identify where
further evidence is needed before applying the findings to larger problems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Prior work and purpose}

\begin{SNcolumns}

Reinforcement learning studies decisions whose consequences become visible
through experience. A policy chooses an action from the information available
at the time. The environment responds, and the learner uses that outcome to
improve later choices. The challenge is to learn useful behavior from limited
experience when observations are incomplete, outcomes are noisy, and rewards
may arrive much later.

Research has developed several ways to address that challenge. Value-based
methods estimate the future reward of each action~\cite{dqn,doubleq}.
Distributional methods retain information about the range of possible
outcomes~\cite{c51,qrdqn}. Policy-gradient methods improve the action rule
directly~\cite{ppo,gae}. Other work considers learning from historical data,
using a model to imagine future experience, or learning objectives from human
comparisons~\cite{cql,dreamerv3,preferences}. Each changes part of the learning
problem; none removes the need for relevant observations and reliable feedback.

Our work brings these approaches into a common experimental setting. The
purpose is to change how decisions are learned while preserving what the
observations, actions, and outcomes mean. This allows us to investigate a
failure before deciding that a different algorithm is needed. A policy may
lack history, receive the wrong reward, or spend its computation repeatedly
examining similar choices.

We examine three connected questions. How should a limited evaluation budget
be allocated? Can the same learning methods handle decisions involving a
variable set of objects and constraints? And how should recent history,
delayed rewards, and user preferences enter the learning process? Small
controlled examples make the mechanisms visible. Their results establish
what happened in those examples, rather than a general ranking of methods.

\section{Preserving the meaning of experience}

\subsection{The information available at the decision}

A useful record connects four things: what the decision-maker knew, which
actions were possible, what it chose, and what followed. The ordering matters.
Information revealed after a choice may be used to assess it, but cannot be
included among the observations that supposedly produced it.

Some problems need more than the latest observation. If a service's reliability
changes, a recent history of successes and failures can inform the next call.
If two situations look identical in the recorded input but require different
actions, a larger model alone cannot recover the missing distinction.
The representation must supply the relevant information first.

Distributed learning adds another issue. Experience may have been collected
by an older policy. IMPALA studies how to correct learning when the programs
collecting experience and the learner progress at different rates~\cite{impala}.
Our setting similarly retains the policy responsible for each choice and the
sequence in which events occurred. Reordering unrelated observations cannot
reconstruct an episode.

\subsection{Separating learning from evaluation}

Training examples change the policy. Evaluation examples measure it. This
separation must include any preprocessing learned from the data, such as the
scale used to normalize observations. It must also respect the unit of
experience: placing different steps of the same simulated path in both groups
can reveal information shared by that path.

We assign episodes to their roles before collection and evaluate without
updating the policy. A comparison also needs a declared budget and a rule for
choosing the model to report. Once evaluation results influence further tuning,
fresh reserved cases are needed for a new confirmation.

\subsection{Different sources of uncertainty}

A noisy observation, an unpredictable outcome, and a poorly estimated model
are different problems. A distributional value model describes variation in
returns; its spread is not automatically a measure of how little the model
knows. A policy trained only on historical examples may assign optimistic
values to actions scarcely represented in them. Conservative Q-learning was
developed to address that latter problem~\cite{cql}.

Entropy-based learning encourages a range of actions during training.
Eysenbach and Levine relate it to robustness under specified classes of
reward and transition disturbances~\cite{maxentrobust}. That connection gives
a reason to test it under disturbances, not a guarantee against every kind
of noise. We therefore vary observation noise explicitly in the comparison
below and report the conditions under which the result was obtained.

\end{SNcolumns}

\section{Choosing where to spend computation}
\label{sec:qubo}

\subsection{Promising choices can repeat the same mistake}

When evaluating a possible future is expensive, a search procedure must decide
which possibilities deserve further work. Ranking them by a cheap estimate
is a natural starting point. But the highest estimates may all describe
closely related choices. If their shared assumption is wrong, the entire
budget can be spent exploring the same unhelpful region.

Prioritized replay uses an estimate of learning value to select past
experience~\cite{per}. Related research formulates episode selection as a
binary optimization problem~\cite{quboepisodes}. We apply a similar idea
earlier in a search: select a small set of promising paths while penalizing
similarity within that set. This favors both estimated quality and diversity.
The binary formulation is commonly called QUBO, or quadratic unconstrained
binary optimization. Our experiment uses an ordinary classical search method;
no quantum computation is involved.

For a fixed number of selected paths, the objective is simply
\[
 \text{selection score}
 = \text{total estimated usefulness}
 - \lambda\,\text{total pairwise similarity}.
\]
The coefficient $\lambda$ controls how much diversity is worth sacrificing
estimated quality for. At each stage, retained paths are expanded and the
next subset is chosen. Only the final retained paths receive the expensive
evaluation. The usefulness estimates must therefore be available cheaply
\emph{before} that evaluation.

\subsection{What the search experiment showed}

We compared this selection rule with keeping only the highest estimates on
500 paired synthetic trees. Each tree contained 4,096 complete paths. The
estimates were constructed from the known best outcome beneath each branch,
then corrupted with noise shared by related paths and with independent noise.
This deliberately favorable source of estimates isolates the effect of
selection; it does not show how accurately a practical model would predict
those outcomes.

The comparison measures how often search finds the best path and how far its
chosen result falls short when it misses. We call that shortfall the
\emph{gap to the optimum}. Lower is better.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Search quality and selection cost over the same 500 trees.
The cost column excludes the final path evaluations.}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Selection rule & Paths retained & Best path found & Mean gap & Selection time \\
\midrule
Highest estimates & 8 & 72.2\% & 0.1435 & 0.09 ms \\
Estimates with diversity & 8 & 73.2\% & 0.1305 & 2.87 ms \\
Highest estimates & 10 & 74.4\% & 0.1266 & 0.11 ms \\
Estimates with diversity & 10 & 75.4\% & 0.1135 & 3.57 ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

With eight retained paths, diversity reduced the mean gap by 0.0130, with a
paired 95\% interval of $[0.0022,0.0239]$. At some other budgets the interval
included zero. Keeping six paths slightly reduced the mean gap but found the
exact best path less often. Diversity therefore helped average quality in
this experiment without improving every measure at every budget.

The added selection cost matters. Retaining eight diverse paths gave roughly
similar, though slightly worse, aggregate quality to retaining the ten highest
estimates. It avoided two final evaluations and thirty-two cheap candidate
calculations, at about 2.75 ms of extra selection work on the test machine.
Ignoring the candidate savings, each avoided final evaluation would need to
cost more than about 1.38 ms to recover that overhead. A practical comparison
must include the cost of obtaining the estimates as well.

\section{Representing objects and feasible choices}

\begin{SNcolumns}

Many applications do not have a fixed list of actions. The available jobs,
resources, or instruments can change, and choosing one option can alter which
others remain possible. Representing everything as an unchanging vector can
obscure those relationships.

Deep Sets explains how to learn from a collection without making its arbitrary
row order meaningful~\cite{deepsets}. Graph networks extend the representation
to relationships between objects~\cite{graphnet}. In our setting, the policy
receives the current objects and candidate actions, while the application
supplies their meaning and feasibility. Reordering the list should leave the
underlying choice unchanged.

A sequence of choices also needs to retain its earlier selections. Assigning
one worker may consume capacity needed by the next. The policy can condition
its preference on what has already been chosen, while a separate feasibility
rule removes invalid alternatives. This distinction lets learning rank the
permitted choices without asking it to rediscover known constraints.

The experiments below test whether this representation preserves learning on
a familiar task and whether examples help when several constrained choices
must be made together.

\end{SNcolumns}

\section{Comparing learners}

A learning curve should answer a practical question: does the policy make
better decisions as it gains experience? Training loss alone cannot answer
this. A value learner predicts future reward; a policy learner changes the
probability of actions. Their losses measure different quantities and cannot
be used as a common league table.

We compare the reward earned on episodes excluded from training, with equal
interaction budgets and several independent training runs. The first shows
performance on new episodes; the second reveals sensitivity to initialization.
Neither establishes performance on a different problem. Jörmungandr records
these evaluations against the model version that produced them, alongside the
training statistics needed to investigate failures.

\subsection{Learning speed and uncertainty}

We compared a standard value-learning method (DQN) with distributional and
entropy-based methods. C51 represents possible returns with probabilities on fixed points,
while QR-DQN learns quantiles~\cite{c51,qrdqn}. Soft-Q and soft actor-critic (SAC)
encourage a spread of action probabilities during learning~\cite{sac}. These
are different approaches to the same decision problem, so evaluation uses
the reward each policy earns rather than its training loss.

We compared these approaches on a synthetic spread that fluctuates around a stable level. Five
training runs per method received the same interaction budget and were
assessed on the same sixteen reserved paths. A second experiment added noise
to the observations. Figure~\ref{fig:ou-algorithm-convergence} shows how reward
changed through training.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ou_algorithm_convergence.pdf}
  \caption{Reward on paths excluded from training, with clean and noisy
  observations. The dashed line is a fixed decision rule. Shading gives
  normal 95\% intervals across five training runs on the same evaluation paths.}
  \label{fig:ou-algorithm-convergence}
\end{figure}

Soft-Q had the highest final mean on clean observations, and categorical SAC
had the highest mean with noisy observations. Several uncertainty intervals
overlap. The useful finding is that all five methods learned this task, with
different learning curves; these runs do not identify a general winner.
The intervals describe variation across five training runs on fixed evaluation
paths, not uncertainty across markets or environments.

\subsection{Checking an implementation against a reference}

Proximal policy optimization (PPO) updates a policy using actions collected
under an earlier version of that policy. Its clipped objective discourages excessively large probability
changes during an update~\cite{ppo}. Before using it on unfamiliar tasks, we
compared it with an independently maintained reference~\cite{sb3}.

In CartPole, the agent moves a cart left or right to keep a pole upright.
Both implementations reached the maximum evaluation return of 500 in all
three runs. The reference reached the declared solved threshold earlier
in two runs. This checks the basic learning path against a reference; it
does not establish equivalence on larger tasks.

We then represented the same observations as named entities and the two
actions as candidates. This matters when an application has a varying number
of objects or choices. The Deep Sets paper explains why arbitrary row order
should not change the meaning of a set~\cite{deepsets}; graph networks extend
the discussion to relationships between objects~\cite{graphnet}. Our
structured policy also reached 500 in each run, and reordering rows preserved
the selected action. Representation could therefore change without losing
the behavior learned on this simple problem.

\subsection{Learning from examples and respecting constraints}

Behavior cloning learns to imitate supplied decisions. It can give a policy
a useful starting point before reinforcement learning adjusts those decisions
according to their outcomes. The quality of that starting point depends on
the examples: copying a teacher successfully does not show that the teacher
makes good decisions.

Our small work-allocation task makes this distinction measurable. Several
workers choose jobs subject to shared capacity and conflicting assignments.
The task is small enough to enumerate every feasible solution and calculate
the best total reward. A value of one in
Figure~\ref{fig:constrained-workbench} therefore means the exact optimum.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/constrained_workbench_j1.pdf}
  \caption{Reward as a fraction of the best feasible result in the
  work-allocation task. Lines average three training runs; shading is one
  standard deviation. BC denotes learning from examples alone. The other
  fixed reference chooses randomly among feasible actions.}
  \label{fig:constrained-workbench}
\end{figure}

Imitation alone earned 0.9080 of the optimum. Further PPO training raised the
mean to 0.9227; PPO starting from a random policy reached 0.8750 under the
same interaction budget. Good demonstrations helped here, though the gain
from additional reinforcement learning was modest.

Feasibility is supplied separately. An \emph{action mask} removes choices
that the environment says are invalid before the policy samples an action.
Huang and Ontañón explain this construction for policy-gradient
methods~\cite{actionmask}. In a simulated taxi task, which requires collecting and delivering a
passenger, every masked method avoided
invalid choices. Our masked PPO completed 92--100\% of evaluation tasks across
three runs, while the unmasked version completed none. The independent masked
references reached 100\% in all runs. Our policy also regressed after an earlier
stage of training: satisfying constraints did not ensure stable learning.

These examples separate three questions: can a method learn the task, does its
representation preserve the choices, and is the resulting policy useful?
Learning a simple task and preserving action identity are necessary checks.
Usefulness must still be assessed in the application itself.

\begin{SNcolumns}

\section{Learning from history and feedback}
\label{sec:feedback}

\subsection{A language model choosing tools}

Suppose several services can perform the same job, but their reliability
changes. Choosing well requires recent evidence: which service was called,
what it returned, and whether that result was correct. A pretrained model
cannot know those local changes unless the application supplies the history.

We tested whether a small language model could learn this choice. Its input contained the available services and sixteen recent calls;
an independent verifier checked each result. We adapted a small pretrained
model~\cite{smollm2} using LoRA, a method that learns compact adjustments
while retaining the original model weights~\cite{lora}.
The model selected a service; the service performed the job.

The learning target was the reward for the chosen call, with no future-reward
term. The task therefore tests the next choice, rather than long-term planning. During evaluation, model weights stayed fixed while the recorded
history changed. The policy could respond to a recent failure through its
input, without being retrained after each call.

Across seven reserved tasks, two trained models stayed within 2.002 percentage
points of the strongest matched reference in the worst case. These references
used simple rules based on recent outcomes. The result shows that the language
policy learned the tested routing task; it does not show an advantage over
those simpler rules. The jobs and their input types were already known, so
this was also not a test of discovering how an arbitrary API works.
The linked study in the final source note gives the task-by-task comparisons.

\subsection{An extra learning objective must earn its place}

A model can be trained to predict an observation as well as choose an action.
The hope is that prediction teaches it something useful for the decision.
Success on the extra task, however, is only an intermediate result: the policy
must still improve.

Motivated by the context-selection idea in ContextRL~\cite{contextrl}, we
ran smaller numeric experiments in which a model had to distinguish histories
supporting different statements about recent outcomes. We also tested
forecasting noisy observations. These were separate experiments, not a
replication of ContextRL's language-model training.

The extra objectives learned their assigned tasks but brought little benefit
to decisions. In the noisy-observation study, final reward gains were about
0.02--0.06 percentage points, with no sustained early-learning advantage.
We therefore leave these objectives off by default. They remain available
for tasks where their added training cost produces a measurable improvement.

\subsection{Connecting a later outcome to an earlier decision}

A reward may arrive only after a sequence of actions. The learner then has
to determine which earlier choices contributed to it. One-step value learning
passes information backward through successive updates. A multi-step return
includes several observed rewards in the same target, allowing a distant
outcome to influence an earlier choice more directly.

The choice involves a tradeoff. Longer returns use more observed outcomes
and depend less on an immediate value estimate, but can be noisier. If the
intermediate actions came from a different policy, they can also introduce
bias. Generalized Advantage Estimation develops a related tradeoff for
policy-gradient learning by weighting information from different
horizons~\cite{gae}.

We isolated delay with one meaningful choice followed by forced waits. At a
twelve-step delay, initial-choice accuracy after 128 updates was 85.9\% for
one-step DQN and about 94\% for four- and thirteen-step returns. All methods
used the same recorded episodes. With immediate rewards, one-step learning
was faster initially; after 512 updates, performance was similar.
Figure~\ref{fig:delayed-feedback} shows that the benefit here was earlier
learning under delay, rather than a higher final accuracy.

\end{SNcolumns}

A late network response is a different problem. Its result belongs to the
original action, even if other actions have since occurred. The application
must match it to that action before submitting finalized experience. Treating
an unknown outcome as zero reward would change what the model learns.

\subsection{Learning what a user prefers}

Two answers can both be correct while one is more useful to a particular
reader. A preference model learns that relative judgment. Christiano and
colleagues demonstrated learning from comparisons between short behavior
sequences~\cite{preferences}. Our smaller study asks how to rank a shortlist
of candidate answers from feedback.

An explicit comparison says which of two options the user prefers. A click
says which option was selected, but may also reflect its position or whether
the user noticed the alternatives. We therefore record what was displayed,
in what order, and what feedback followed. An unselected answer is not
automatically labeled wrong.

The scorer learns from score differences: increasing the score of the
preferred item relative to the other reduces its training loss. These scores
express preference, not the probability that an answer is correct. They also
have a different purpose from a distributional critic such as C51, which
models possible future returns~\cite{c51}.

In a simulation with six candidates, three were acceptable under a known
utility function. After training on 1,024 events, explicit comparisons
recovered 97.0\% of acceptable items in the top three. Position-biased clicks
recovered 57.2\%; randomizing display order raised this to 75.4\%.
The simulated click behavior was deliberately biased, so these figures
are not estimates of real users' behavior. Comparisons also require different
human effort from clicks. The result illustrates why feedback collection is
part of the learning problem.

\end{SNcolumns}

\begin{SNcolumns}

Jörmungandr supplies the records and preference loss. Applications decide
what to display and how to interpret feedback. The current preference library
keeps its pending records in memory; persistent storage and an HTTP feedback
service remain to be built.

\section{What the results establish}

The comparisons show that the runtime can train and serve several kinds of
policy, preserve structured choices, and support experiments with delayed
outcomes and user feedback. They also exposed weaknesses, including the
late regression in Taxi and auxiliary objectives that added cost with little
benefit. The recorded results should guide which features an application uses.

The experiments are deliberately small. They do not establish performance
across the much broader tasks studied in the cited literature, and no trading
performance follows from them. The delayed-reward study isolates one opening
choice; the preference study uses simulated users; the search study supplies
estimates derived from the known optimum. Each makes one mechanism easier to
study by leaving out difficulties that a practical application must face.

The next tests should introduce those difficulties separately: estimates
learned from observable data, several consequential decisions before a reward,
and feedback from real users. This would show whether the benefits survive
outside the controlled settings and whether they repay their computational
or human cost.

\section{Conclusion}

Jörmungandr lets an application change its learner while retaining the same
collection, evaluation, and serving interfaces. That made it possible to
compare methods and investigate specific learning problems without rebuilding
the surrounding system.

The studies suggest practical choices: supply relevant history, evaluate an
extra objective by the decisions it improves, match return horizons to reward
delay, and distinguish explicit preference from a click. Each finding has a
limited experimental scope. The next language-model study needs fresh tasks,
since the current confirmation cases have already informed development.

\end{SNcolumns}
